# From quantum channels to a clock for irreversible decay

The educational anchor is **John Watrous, *The Theory of Quantum Information* (Cambridge University Press, 2018)**. The [author's book page](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~watrous/TQI/) identifies the edition and provides its manuscript and errata. Section numbers below were checked against that 2018 manuscript. This bridge uses the book's finite-dimensional channel framework, then develops the additional decay and algorithmic constructions in the project's scientific files.

A familiar starting point is an excited atom with a known decay rate. Sampling its classical waiting time is straightforward. Here the rate acts on an unknown quantum superposition and is supplied through a coherent circuit. Measuring which rate is present can change the state. The simulator must reproduce the quantum channel, including how an unobserved event time changes coherence.

The notation translation is small but important. A vector written $`u`$ in the book may be written $`|\psi\rangle`$ here; its pure-state density operator is $`|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|`$. An adjoint written with a star in the book is written with a dagger here. The project's $`A`$ always denotes total loss, while a general Stinespring operator in the book need not have that meaning. Likewise, the jump $`J_a`$ is not the book's Choi representation of a channel.

## The channel and its record

The model has jump operators $`J_a`$, a stacked map $`L`$, and a positive loss matrix $`A`$:

```math
L=\sum_a|a\rangle\otimes J_a,
\qquad A=L^\dagger L=\sum_aJ_a^\dagger J_a\le I.
```

There is no Hamiltonian term. The required channel is generated by

```math
\mathcal L(\rho)=\sum_aJ_a\rho J_a^\dagger
-\frac12(A\rho+\rho A),
\qquad \mathcal E_t=e^{t\mathcal L}.
```

The positive term transfers an event's output into the system. The two negative terms supply the corresponding no-event loss. The complete evolution preserves trace even though a single conditional branch generally does not.

For a finite channel with Kraus operators $`K_x`$, the same operation has two useful descriptions:

```math
\Phi(\rho)=\sum_xK_x\rho K_x^\dagger,
\qquad
V=\sum_x|x\rangle\otimes K_x,
\qquad
\Phi(\rho)=\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{record}}(V\rho V^\dagger).
```

The condition $`\sum_xK_x^\dagger K_x=I`$ says that $`V`$ is an isometry. In the second description, the record is retained until the final trace. Conditional branches with orthogonal record values contribute a sum to the reduced channel. Replacing those branches by a coherent sum of their Kraus operators would insert cross terms and usually change the operation. This application of the representations in Section 2.2.2 is the foundation for the project's event clock.

## Unequal rates make the record visible

Consider two excited states and two dark destination states. They decay through **one shared jump channel**,

```math
J=\frac12|g_a\rangle\langle e_a|
+|g_b\rangle\langle e_b|.
```

The amplitudes are $`1/2`$ and $`1`$, so the corresponding rates are $`\lambda_a=1/4`$ and $`\lambda_b=1`$. Prepare

```math
|+_e\rangle=\frac{|e_a\rangle+|e_b\rangle}{\sqrt2}.
```

The two branches emit at different typical times. The multiplier transferring the input off-diagonal matrix element into the ground-state off-diagonal element is the overlap of their event-time amplitudes:

```math
c_{ab}(t)
=\int_0^t\sqrt{\lambda_a\lambda_b}
e^{-s(\lambda_a+\lambda_b)/2}\,ds
=\frac{2\sqrt{\lambda_a\lambda_b}}{\lambda_a+\lambda_b}
\left(1-e^{-t(\lambda_a+\lambda_b)/2}\right).
```

At long times this coefficient tends to $`4/5=0.8`$. The input off-diagonal entry was $`1/2`$, so the final entry is $`0.4`$, not $`0.8`$. On the ground-state basis, the final state is

```math
\rho_\infty=\frac12
\begin{pmatrix}1&0.8\\0.8&1\end{pmatrix}.
```

For a measurement projecting onto $`|+_g\rangle=(|g_a\rangle+|g_b\rangle)/\sqrt2`$, the outcome probability is $`0.9`$. A construction that combined all emitted events into one Kraus operator would instead preserve the full ground-state superposition and predict probability one. It would get both final populations right while getting the channel wrong. The output-state difference has unhalved trace norm $`0.2`$, so the two channels are separated by at least that amount in unhalved diamond norm.

The [full example](../science/examples.md#two-excited-levels-with-unequal-lifetimes) gives the finite-time coefficients and numerical checks. The distinction disappears for equal rates in this shared-channel example, because the two waiting-time shapes then coincide. With separate physical jump labels, those labels would themselves reveal the branch; the shared-channel assumption is therefore part of the calculation.

## Why the error contract includes a reference

The caller may supply a system entangled with another register. The simulator must act correctly on its part without knowing or modifying that reference. For a difference of channels $`\Delta=\widetilde\Phi-\Phi`$, the relevant norm can be written as

```math
\|\Delta\|_\diamond
=\sup_{\rho_{SR}}
\| (\Delta\otimes\mathrm{id}_R)(\rho_{SR})\|_1,
```

with a reference of input dimension sufficient. This is the book's completely bounded trace norm, written here with the usual diamond symbol. **No factor of one half is included.** For equal-prior channel discrimination, success probability is $`1/2+\|\Phi-\Psi\|_\diamond/4`$; a norm of two corresponds to perfect distinction.

Sections 3.3.2–3.3.3 supply the operational norm framework. The project repeatedly uses its stability under adding an untouched reference and composing channels. For example, replacing one component of a channel circuit changes the complete channel by no more than that component's diamond error; successive replacements add their errors. Proposition 3.48 gives this composition rule in the author-hosted manuscript.

The compiler proves a stronger intermediate statement: its initialized-workspace isometry is close in operator norm to the intended isometry. If $`\|V-W\|\le r`$, tracing their records gives channels at unhalved diamond distance at most $`2r`$. Tensoring with an untouched reference does not alter this bound. Thus the proof controls coherence and reference correlations, not just populations in selected tests.

## From a first-event isometry to a finite clock

For a remaining horizon $`u`$, the no-event operator is $`e^{-uA/2}`$. The amplitude of a first event at time $`s`$ is $`L e^{-sA/2}`$. The exact first-event map therefore satisfies

```math
V_u|\psi\rangle
=|\mathrm{no}\rangle e^{-uA/2}|\psi\rangle
+\int_0^u|\mathrm{yes},s\rangle L e^{-sA/2}|\psi\rangle\,ds,
```

```math
V_u^\dagger V_u
=e^{-uA}+\int_0^u A e^{-sA}\,ds=I.
```

The continuous record is an analytical device. The actual circuit uses a finite binary clock. On a mesh $`\delta`$, it replaces each time interval by a uniform wave packet and uses the left-endpoint stack $`M_\delta(u)`$. The [first-event proof](../science/first_event.md) establishes

```math
I\le M_\delta(u)^\dagger M_\delta(u)\le(1+\delta)I,
\qquad
\|E_\delta M_\delta(u)-V_u\|\le\delta/2.
```

Here $`E_\delta`$ is the comparison embedding of a finite clock label into its time interval. The bound is uniform in $`u`$: a long horizon does not multiply this mesh error. The derivative identity behind it is

```math
\int_0^u
\left(\frac{d}{ds}L e^{-sA/2}\right)^\dagger
\left(\frac{d}{ds}L e^{-sA/2}\right)ds
=\frac14A^2(I-e^{-uA})\le\frac14I.
```

Polar normalization turns the finite stack into an exact ideal grid isometry. The algorithm approximates that isometry by amplifying the entire stack. It does not postselect a physical input on a favorable event or discard failed numerical branches. The construction and its error estimates are project derivations beyond the textbook's channel representations.

## The dyadic compiler and its paid access

A large clock contains many time bins. Giving every time bin the same preparation weight would introduce a costly normalization. The useful observation is that on a band $`[a,2a)`$,

```math
\|\sqrt a\,L e^{-sA/2}\|\le e^{-1/2}
\quad(s\ge a).
```

The first band $`[0,1)`$ is handled separately. In a later band, a uniform superposition over its $`a/\delta`$ grid values contributes amplitude $`\sqrt{\delta/a}`$. Multiplying its operator by $`\sqrt a`$ produces exactly the desired $`\sqrt\delta`$ event amplitude. There are only logarithmically many bands. Their common normalization is the square root of their count, rather than the square root of elapsed time.

The [dyadic compiler](../science/dyadic_compiler.md) combines this weighting with Gaussian functional filters, a coherent remaining-time register and unconditional amplification. The [filter construction](../science/filters_and_resources.md) uses finite uniform random-index registers and reversible arithmetic; it does not assume a supplied Gaussian state or a QRAM table. Singular-value transformation, Hamiltonian simulation and Fourier LCUs are inherited research-level primitives, attributed there and in [related work](../references/related_work.md). They are not algorithms supplied by the chosen textbook.

The oracle is a controlled projected unitary encoding of $`L`$, together with its inverse. These calls are counted. Building that encoding, controlling it, loading its coefficients and synthesizing its gates can be expensive and must be paid. The caller's input preparation and application-specific measurement are additional costs. A concise oracle description does not remove those tasks.

## From one waiting period to the complete theorem

The structural promise is a grading from zero through $`k`$: each jump strictly lowers it, and $`[A,G]=0`$. The second condition keeps waiting from raising the grade again. These two conditions bound the number of genuine events by $`k`$ even for an initial superposition of grades. Nilpotency of a jump by itself does not establish this property; the [counterexample](../science/examples.md#strictly-lowering-jumps-can-still-have-arbitrarily-long-event-records) shows the role of the no-event evolution.

After an event at a recorded grid time, the circuit subtracts that time reversibly and calls the next first-event circuit. All older records remain stored. They are discarded only after the full trajectory has been completed. The [time discretization proof](../science/time_discretization.md) bounds the complete channel error by

```math
(4k+2)\delta+k\zeta,
```

where $`\zeta`$ is the per-slot diamond implementation error. Choosing mesh and circuit accuracies of order $`\varepsilon/(k+1)`$ controls the total error without a factor counting all possible histories.

The resulting [simulation theorem](../science/simulation_theorem.md) has query count

```math
O\!\left[(k+1)\sqrt{1+\tau}\,
\mathrm{polylog}\!\left(
\frac{(2+\tau)(k+1)}{\varepsilon}\right)\right].
```

The normalized horizon is $`\tau=\beta^2T`$ for physical jumps encoded with normalization $`\beta`$ and physical time $`T`$. The bound concerns the full system channel, assumes zero Hamiltonian, and includes arbitrary reference-entangled inputs. It does not promise an externally accessible continuous-time field.

The square-root horizon power cannot be improved uniformly in this access model. For $`\tau\ge1`$ and $`J_\sigma=\sigma|0\rangle\langle1|`$, amplitudes zero and $`\tau^{-1/2}`$ have close oracle unitaries but produce decay probabilities zero and $`1-e^{-1}`$ at time $`\tau`$. The [lower bound](../science/lower_bound.md) turns this difference into $`\Omega(\sqrt\tau)`$ coherent queries. This is an unknown-amplitude oracle statement. A known scalar rate provided as an ordinary number is easy to process classically.

This project derives the waiting-time compiler, its graded recursion and its resource theorem, using separately attributed research primitives.
